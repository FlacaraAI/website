# Flacara — Brand Brief

## The product

Flacara is a dermatoscope built around the iPhone. A housing seats over the phone's camera array and supplies the light: polarized and unpolarized LEDs that alternate at the press of a button, charged over USB-C. There is no added lens. The ~40-micron resolution comes from the phone's own sensor and the software behind it. Every competing phone attachment clips onto a single lens and adds optics to reach its magnification. Flacara doesn't, which is why it costs what it costs.

Two versions ship from the same imaging system.

**Consumer, $199.** Polarized light only. Functionally a very good flashlight with software behind it: a clear, repeatable image of a mole and a record of how it changes. It makes no diagnostic claim, deliberately, and that is what keeps it outside FDA clearance.

**Dermatologist, $249.** Both lighting modes, meant to be used as a working diagnostic instrument. An FDA pre-submission is in progress.

## The workflow it replaces

A dermatologist looks at a lesion through a dermatoscope, at one moment in time, and decides from that whether it needs to be cut. When the lesion isn't clearly benign or malignant, the record of it is usually a sentence of description. Photographing it means clipping the dermatoscope to a phone with a plate, taking the shot, then moving the file through two or three pieces of software before it lands anywhere useful. Location is written down in words. Comparing a lesion across visits is enough friction that it often doesn't happen.

Flacara collapses that. The device that visualizes the lesion is the device that images it. Comparison happens on the phone. Upload and retrieval run through one place instead of several. Location stops being prose: a patented technique maps each lesion spatially onto the body, so finding it again means looking at where it is rather than parsing someone's description of where it was. At $249 a practice can put one in every exam room rather than carrying a single $1,695 instrument between them. And because the consumer version produces the same class of image, a patient can send a dermatologist something actually worth looking at.

## Market position

DermLite DL4 is $1,695. DL5, $1,895. DL5 Plus, $1,995. Heine sits in the same range. The handyscope, the nearest phone-based product, is $795 and uses optics for 10x. Flacara is $199 and $249.

That gap is the brand's central problem. At roughly an eighth the price of the instrument it replaces, Flacara gets read as the cheap one unless the brand has already argued otherwise. There's no help from the category: the other phone-based products, iBoolo's DE-300 and DE-400 among them, look perfectly credible. A low price doesn't read as innovation on its own.

The job is to make the price legible as the result of a different engineering approach rather than a lesser product. Anything the brand touches should look like it came off the same shelf as the $1,695 device.

## Where credibility comes from

Side-by-side against a DermLite DL4, image quality was comparable. That hasn't been quantified, and the brand shouldn't claim more than has been measured. Prototypes are with clinicians; the company sits between prototype and pilot.

The team is small and doesn't come from optics. Its authority is real but borrowed: from the clinicians it's partnered with, and from images anyone can look at and judge for themselves. Flacara should never posture as a lens company. It should show pictures.

## How it should feel

Precise and calm. The brand's competence has to be visible in how carefully it's made, not asserted in what it says about itself — restraint reads as expensive here, and overselling reads as a startup with something to prove.

Two people are on the other end of this device and they need different things from the same voice.

A dermatologist wants to know it's rigorous enough to sit next to real equipment and hold up in a regulatory conversation. Give them density: measurements, methods, the honest limits of what's been tested. Don't simplify for them.

A person checking a spot on their own arm is often frightened. They need steady, not cheerful, and never alarmed. The consumer product cannot interpret anything, so the language can't either — no scores, no risk framing, no implied verdict. What it offers is a clear picture and a way to notice change, which is enough, and saying it plainly is more reassuring than reassurance.

Someone reading both should be able to tell they came from the same company without being told.

## What it isn't

The no-optics approach is how the product got cheap, not what the brand is about. It's a footnote in the engineering story, not the headline.

Hospital-white sterility is the wrong kind of clinical. So is wellness-app softness: pastels, roundness, encouragement.

Nothing about the presentation should acknowledge the price as a selling point. Accessible is a consequence. Discount is a category.

## Fixed and open

The name is locked. No visual system exists yet — color, typography, logotype, and iconography all follow from this document rather than being decided in it. Prices are directional and may move.
